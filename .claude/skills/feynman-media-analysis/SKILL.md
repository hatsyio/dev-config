# Feynman Media Analysis

## Purpose

Transform passive consumption into active understanding.

The skill should help the user move from:

> “I watched/read this work.”

into:

> “I can explain what happens, why it happens, what ideas the work explores, how it communicates them, and where the limits of my interpretation are.”

The goal is **not** to:

- find every symbol;
- discover a hidden “correct” interpretation;
- demonstrate intellectual sophistication;
- impose a particular reading;
- turn every work into an academic exercise.

The goal is to construct a progressively better mental model of the work.

## Feynman principle

If we think we understand something, we should be able to explain it in simple language.

When abstract concepts appear — for example identity, power, freedom, trauma, alienation, class, morality, capitalism, love, sacrifice, control, nihilism, and similar ideas — do not accept the label as the explanation.

Ask:

> What exactly do you mean by that here?

Then ask:

> What concrete elements of the work lead you to that conclusion?

## Fundamental rule

The user should build their interpretation first.

Do not begin by giving a complete analysis of the work.

Work primarily through questions.

The assistant may:

- reformulate ideas;
- detect contradictions;
- introduce useful conceptual vocabulary;
- point out connections;
- propose hypotheses;
- offer alternative interpretations;
- provide external context when useful.

But do this only after giving the user a real opportunity to reason independently.

## Core flow

The central analysis loop is:

1. Understand.
2. Explain.
3. Connect.
4. Abstract.
5. Formulate a thesis.
6. Try to refute it.
7. Refine it.
8. Synthesize it.

These phases are not a rigid checklist.

## Conversation rhythm

Normally ask **one main question at a time**.

Avoid dumping long lists of questions on the user.

After each answer:

1. identify what the user is claiming;
2. separate facts from inferences;
3. detect gaps or contradictions;
4. identify the most fertile next question;
5. continue from there.

Follow the interesting connections that emerge instead of mechanically completing every analysis layer.

## Layer 1 — Reconstruction

Before interpreting, establish what the user understands happened.

Possible questions:

- What actually happens?
- What is the central conflict?
- What changes between the beginning and the end?
- Which events are indispensable?
- What part of the plot remains unclear?
- What would you need to explain to someone who has just finished the work?

Do not search for symbolism yet.

## Layer 2 — Causality

Construct cause → effect chains.

Questions:

- Why does this happen?
- Which decision produces that consequence?
- What could have prevented the outcome?
- Which event genuinely changes the direction of the story?
- What seems important but actually causes very little?

Try to reconstruct:

A → causes B → changes C → leads to D.

## Layer 3 — Characters

Analyze characters as agents with their own mental models.

Ask:

- What does this character want?
- What do they need even if they do not know it?
- What do they fear?
- What do they believe is happening?
- What do they know?
- What do they not know?
- What are they trying to hide?
- What do they say they want?
- What do their actions show they actually want?

Distinguish intention from outcome.

## Layer 4 — Relationships

Many ideas become clearer between characters than inside isolated characters.

Ask:

- What does A want from B?
- What does A believe about B?
- Who has more power?
- What dependency exists?
- What changes in the relationship?
- Does one character function as a contrast to another?

Look for narrative pairs when useful.

## Layer 5 — Subtext

Separate explicit content from implicit interaction.

Ask:

- What are they literally talking about?
- What are they really negotiating?
- Which emotions remain unspoken?
- What is one character trying to make the other feel or do?
- What does the audience know that one of them does not?

Do not assume every conversation contains meaningful subtext.

## Layer 6 — Patterns

Look for recurrence before assigning meaning.

Patterns may include:

- situations;
- phrases;
- objects;
- images;
- behaviors;
- decisions;
- relationships;
- sounds;
- structures;
- mechanics.

Ask first:

> What repeats?

Then:

> Why might that matter?

Never reverse that order.

## Layer 7 — Contrasts

Look for deliberate oppositions.

Abstract examples include:

- freedom / control;
- individual / group;
- reality / appearance;
- knowledge / ignorance;
- desire / duty;
- tradition / change.

Do not impose these pairs. Derive them from the work.

Ask:

> Which two elements does the work seem to want us to compare?

## Layer 8 — Themes

Move from concrete events to general questions.

A theme should not be expressed as a single word.

Avoid:

> “The theme is freedom.”

Prefer:

> “The work explores what happens when the pursuit of security begins to limit freedom.”

Questions:

- What human problem appears repeatedly?
- What question does the work seem to explore?
- Which different positions appear in response to it?
- Does the work seem to offer an answer?
- Or does it keep the question open?

## Layer 9 — Thesis

Once enough evidence exists, ask for a provisional interpretation.

Useful formats:

> The work explores how ______ can cause ______.

> The work seems to ask whether ______.

> The work contrasts ______ and ______ to show ______.

> I think the work is mainly about ______ because ______.

A thesis should connect ideas. It should not be a list of topics.

## Layer 10 — Evidence

A thesis needs evidence.

Look for several kinds where possible:

- actions;
- decisions;
- dialogue;
- character development;
- structure;
- patterns;
- formal choices.

Ask:

> Which three elements of the work best support this reading?

Three is a guideline, not a requirement.

## Layer 11 — Adversarial interpretation

Try to break the thesis.

Ask:

- Which scene does it explain worst?
- Which character does not fit?
- Which detail seems to contradict it?
- What alternative explanation exists?
- Are we ignoring something because we like our interpretation?

A good interpretation should survive serious objections.

## Layer 12 — Refinement

If a contradiction appears, do not defend the thesis at all costs.

It may need to be:

- narrowed;
- broadened;
- combined with another thesis;
- replaced.

A precise thesis is better than one that pretends to explain everything.

## Layer 13 — Form

Analyze how the medium produces meaning when it is relevant.

### Film and television

May include:

- framing;
- composition;
- editing;
- lighting;
- sound;
- music;
- acting;
- pacing;
- color;
- production design;
- point of view.

### Literature

May include:

- narrator;
- focalization;
- style;
- structure;
- language;
- pacing;
- omitted information.

### Video games

May include:

- mechanics;
- player agency;
- rules;
- failure;
- reward;
- interface;
- progression;
- relationship between gameplay and narrative.

### Comics

May include:

- page composition;
- panels;
- visual pacing;
- transitions;
- color;
- relationship between text and image.

Ask:

> Why is this communicated in this particular way rather than another?

## Layer 14 — Audience experience

Analyze how the work manages the reader, viewer, or player’s experience.

Ask:

- What do we know at each moment?
- What do we initially believe?
- When does our interpretation change?
- What information is withheld?
- Which expectation is being used?
- Are we encouraged to judge something before receiving all the information?
- Are we made to experience the same uncertainty as the characters?

## Layer 15 — Realism and narrative function

Do not assume every choice must work realistically.

If a scene feels strange, ask:

- Does it work psychologically?
- Does it work dramatically?
- Does it work thematically?
- Does it work as satire?
- Does it work as allegory?
- Is it simply weak?

The skill must allow the conclusion:

> “This does not fully work.”

Do not rescue every weakness through interpretation.

# Optional lenses

These lenses are **not** mandatory stages.

Activate them only when the work itself provides evidence that they are useful.

Never introduce a lens merely because it exists in this list.

## Agency

Useful when the work contains restrictions, obedience, dependency, decisions, or loss of control.

Ask:

> Who still makes meaningful decisions of their own?

## Identity

Useful when a person strongly defines themselves through a profession, group, ideology, relationship, hobby, reputation, or social role.

Ask:

> Is this something the character does, or something they believe they are?

## Power

Useful for asymmetric relationships.

Analyze:

- who can decide;
- who can punish;
- who depends on whom;
- which power is formal;
- which power is informal.

## Systems and incentives

Useful when individual behavior is shaped by institutions or structures.

Ask:

- What does this environment reward?
- What does it punish?
- What behavior does it produce?
- Who benefits?

Do not automatically reduce systemic problems to personal flaws.

## Morality

Useful when the work contains meaningful ethical decisions.

Ask:

- What does each character consider right?
- Which principle are they sacrificing?
- Do they believe the ends justify the means?
- What responsibility do they carry?

Do not automatically turn analysis into moral judgment.

## Freedom

Useful when conflicts appear between freedom, security, duty, control, or responsibility.

Distinguish freedom from power and agency.

## Work and vocation

Useful when work itself is central.

May explore:

- purpose;
- professional identity;
- sacrifice;
- recognition;
- burnout;
- work-life relationship.

Do not activate merely because characters have jobs.

## Creator and audience

Useful in works about art, culture, production, performance, interpretation, or criticism.

May distinguish:

- creating;
- consuming;
- appreciating;
- evaluating;
- using culture as status.

## Status

Useful when decisions are shaped by prestige, reputation, belonging, or social display.

Ask:

> Would this matter equally if nobody else could see it?

## Group and individual

Useful when the work contains conformity, community, sectarian behavior, social pressure, belonging, or isolation.

## Psychology

Useful when the work relies strongly on perception, memory, self-deception, desire, fear, defensive mechanisms, or identity.

Avoid unnecessary diagnosis of characters.

## Society

Useful when the work clearly engages with social structures.

May include:

- class;
- inequality;
- gender;
- technology;
- institutions;
- consumption;
- culture.

Do not automatically force a sociopolitical reading.

## Philosophy

Useful when the work raises questions about identity, knowledge, reality, morality, freedom, death, meaning, or consciousness.

Prefer simple language when it explains the idea better than philosophical jargon.

# Lens selection

Before using a lens, ask internally:

1. Is there clear evidence in the work?
2. Does it explain something we still do not understand?
3. Does it connect several relevant elements?
4. Does it produce a genuinely useful new question?

If not, discard it.

# Anti-overfitting rule

Never analyze a work through a framework merely because that framework worked well on another work.

Each work should generate its own conceptual map.

Do not assume in advance that questions of status, power, trauma, identity, class, agency, work, systems, or any other lens will be relevant.

These are tools, not conclusions.

# Anti-symbolism rule

Do not search for symbols by default.

Before assigning symbolic meaning to something, check:

1. Does it repeat?
2. Does it receive unusual attention?
3. Does its meaning change?
4. Does it connect with an already-supported theme?
5. Does the symbolic reading explain something that the literal reading does not?

If not, accept that it may simply be an element of the work.

# Anti-intentionality rule

Do not state:

> “The author wanted to say X.”

without evidence.

Prefer:

> “The work supports a reading of X.”

Distinguish among:

- meaning produced by the work;
- the audience’s interpretation;
- documented authorial intent.

# Anti-intellectualization rule

Understanding more does not necessarily mean making the interpretation more complicated.

If a simple explanation explains the work better, prefer it.

Periodically ask:

> Are we understanding the work better, or merely making our interpretation more sophisticated?

# Anti-performance rule

Analysis should not become a performance of intelligence.

Avoid:

- seeking complexity for prestige;
- dismissing simple interpretations;
- inventing symbolism;
- unnecessary jargon;
- competing to find deeper readings.

Analysis should increase:

- understanding;
- enjoyment;
- curiosity;
- explanatory ability.

# Progressive synthesis

During long analyses, periodically stop and ask:

> So far, what do you think we have discovered?

Then:

> Which of these ideas seems most central?

This prevents the analysis from becoming a collection of observations without structure.

# Stop condition

Do not analyze indefinitely.

A session may end when:

- the plot is clear;
- the main motivations are explained;
- a coherent thesis exists;
- its strongest evidence is known;
- at least one limitation or alternative has been identified;
- the user can explain the interpretation in their own words.

Not every ambiguity needs to be resolved.

# Final Feynman test

Ask:

> Explain what this work is really about to an intelligent person who has just finished it but feels they only understood the surface.

A good answer should connect, where relevant:

- conflict;
- causality;
- characters;
- transformation;
- theme;
- form;
- interpretation.

If the answer becomes a list of concepts such as:

> “It is about identity, power, love, and society.”

ask:

> What relationship connects those ideas?

# Transfer

Optional.

If the conversation naturally leads there, ask:

> Has this work changed or refined how you understand anything outside the work itself?

Do not automatically convert artistic analysis into self-help.

Transfer may be:

- intellectual;
- emotional;
- moral;
- practical;
- aesthetic.

It is also perfectly valid to conclude:

> “I do not need to extract a personal lesson; I simply understand the work better now.”

# Modes

## Quick

Approximately 5–8 questions.

Prioritize:

understanding → characters → theme → thesis.

## Deep

Explore while genuinely new connections continue to appear.

Include when useful:

- causality;
- relationships;
- patterns;
- form;
- optional lenses;
- adversarial testing.

## Exhaustive

For especially dense works.

May include:

- multiple interpretations;
- full structural analysis;
- historical context;
- formal analysis;
- external research;
- comparison with other works.

Do not confuse exhaustiveness with inventing meaning.

# External information

By default, begin without consulting:

- reviews;
- analyses;
- interviews;
- ending explanations;
- third-party interpretations.

First construct an independent reading.

Afterward, if the user wants, contrast it with:

- creator statements;
- professional criticism;
- academic reception;
- historical context;
- popular interpretations.

External information should provide comparison, not replace the user’s reasoning.

# Recommended opening

Begin with a question equivalent to:

> Without looking for symbolism or external explanations yet, what do you think actually happens in the work, and what would you say its central conflict is?

From the answer onward, abandon any predetermined route and pursue whichever question is most likely to improve the user’s current mental model.

# Final principle

A good interpretation is not the one that finds the most meanings.

It is the one that can explain clearly and defensibly:

what happens
→ why it happens
→ what the characters want
→ which relationships matter
→ which patterns appear
→ which ideas the work connects
→ how the work communicates them
→ what evidence supports the reading
→ and where the explanation stops working.

The skill should help the user discover that model, not hand it to them prefabricated.
